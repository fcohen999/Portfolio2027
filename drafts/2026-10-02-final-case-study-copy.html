CDC OneLab REACH

An e-learning platform for nurses and first responders

A research deliverable that turned into a $9.2M client relationship

Design Lead — CDC OneLab REACH, an e-learning platform for nurses and first responders, grown from a solo research engagement into a 3-person team.

Client

Centers for Disease Control and Prevention

Role

Design Lead

Year

Product

OneLab REACH, e-learning for nurses and first responders

The Problem

CDC needed an e-learning platform for OneLab REACH to train nurses and first responders — but it also needed to read as a distinct product, not another interchangeable CDC informational site. Generic government visual language works for publishing information; it doesn't work for a platform people are expected to actually engage with for mandatory training.

My Role

I ran this as a two-phase engagement: research first, design second. In phase one, I used quantitative analysis, user testing, and competitive analysis to produce a Report Summary — metrics-backed findings and recommendations, not just a design proposal. The CDC's response to that research quality is what won me the design and implementation phase, and eventually the flexible design system now supporting the platform's ongoing expansion.

Approach

The design system decision was deliberate: I moved away from typical government visual language toward a set of more saturated, CDC brand-adjacent colors, using tints and shades to expand the palette without losing consistency. For a training platform competing for attention against mandatory-compliance fatigue, that distinction mattered more than it would for a purely informational site.

The landing page had to do four things at once — drive sign-ups, cut bounce rate, route users to featured courses, and stay adaptable as content expanded — so I designed it as a system of prioritized entry points rather than a single static layout. Past the landing page, My Learner Hub became the retention mechanism: free course access got users in the door, but creating an account unlocked saved and in-progress content in one place, giving people a reason to convert beyond the content itself.

Outcome

The CDC called the research and design work "high quality" enough to "wow" their own stakeholders and build lasting trust — in their words, foundational to five years of continued work together. Concretely: the relationship generated $9.2M in additional CDC contracts, and I stayed on past the initial engagement, growing from a solo research lead into managing a team of two designers to deliver the follow-on work.

“[Fiona created a] high quality final deliverable for our project that ‘wow’d’ our clients and built trust with them. This research work will be the foundation of future development work with them for the coming 5 years.”

— Claire Douger, CSELS Account Team Manager, CDC

CDC OneLab REACH

An e-learning platform for nurses and first responders

Turning "we don't know how to build this" into a five-project relationship

Design Lead — OneLab REACH, an e-learning platform helping nurses, first responders, and other healthcare professionals find training and earn continuing-education credits.

Client

Centers for Disease Control and Prevention

Role

Design Lead

Product

OneLab REACH, e-learning for nurses and first responders

Engagement

~3.5 years on CDC work; 4+ CDC engagements, brought in by name

CDC didn't know how to approach building an app. I gave them a technical strategy they could understand and act on — and that's what CDC kept coming back for, first for the platform, then for the system behind it, then for the projects after that.

The problem wasn't the platform. It was that no one could see the path to it.

CDC needed nurses, first responders, and other healthcare professionals to be able to find training and earn continuing-education credits — credits tied to federal CLIA requirements, with real expiration dates and real consequences: without them, a physician or lab director can lose the ability to legally perform certain procedures.

The request came from CDC's Center for Surveillance, Epidemiology, and Laboratory Services (CSELS), as it was known at the time — the group responsible for both the research and the app that followed. CDC knew they needed something. They didn't know what, or how to get there. There was no technical team translating the need into a plan — just a real, regulatory-grade problem and a client that couldn't yet tell the difference between a good answer and a bad one.

I was brought in to find that path.

The pitch that had to work in a room with no technical background

Before any screen existed, CDC needed a decision made: what were we actually going to build, and how?

I recommended a web app first, designed mobile-first, with a hybrid native app to follow later — sequencing the highest-reach, lowest-cost path before committing to native development CDC wasn't yet positioned to support.

The harder part wasn't the recommendation. It was that the room deciding whether to trust it had no technical background to evaluate it against. I had to make the reasoning legible without requiring anyone in that room to become technical themselves — what we'd build, why in that order, and what each choice would cost or save later.

It worked. CDC didn't just accept the plan — they came back and asked for more than what I'd pitched: design the web app, and while you're at it, build us a design system.

That moment mattered more than any single screen that came after it. It's the reason CDC handed execution to me instead of routing it to a separate development team.

Designing the part people would actually use

With the direction set, I designed the web app itself — the product CDC and I had agreed on in that room.

The landing page had to do four things at once: let people find training without friction, surface the courses CDC most needed people to take, keep bounce low, and stay flexible enough for CDC to keep adding courses without redesigning the page every time new material came in. I designed it around progressive commitment rather than a gated signup — visitors could see and start exploring the catalog immediately, with featured courses giving CDC a direct lever to surface whatever was timely or high-priority without making people hunt for it. Registration entered the experience at the point where it unlocked something real, not as a wall between the visitor and the content. I also built the page as a durable structure rather than a one-time layout: expandable content areas and reusable card and section patterns so CDC could keep publishing new courses without the homepage needing to be rethought each time.

My Learner Hub was the payoff for that account, and it's where the account strategy actually became visible as an interface. I designed it as the single place a returning user could see courses in progress, resume where they'd left off, and pull certificates — turning the account from a login requirement into the thing that made coming back worth it. Favoriting was the hinge that connected the two halves of the product: someone could discover a course while browsing anonymously, save it with one action, and find it waiting for them the next time they signed in. Progress bars on in-progress courses did real work too — they gave people a visible reason to finish something they'd started rather than letting training sit half-done. Together, favoriting, progress, and certificates formed a loop — discover, save, learn, return, track — that gave the platform a reason to exist beyond any single course.

From brand guidelines to a system other people could build from

The design system started as something closer to brand guidelines — a way to give REACH its own identity, more saturated and product-like than CDC's informational sites, while staying recognizably CDC. I expanded the existing CDC palette into a wider, more saturated set of tints and shades, kept it tight enough to stay disciplined, and reserved specific colors for functional meaning — status, success, warnings — so the system communicated as much as it decorated.

That wasn't the end state CDC needed, though. The platform was going to keep growing — more courses, more content, more designers eventually touching it — so brand guidelines alone weren't enough. I built out actual reusable components on top of that foundation: course cards, status and progress indicators, navigation patterns, page structures that could hold new material without the surrounding product needing to be rethought each time a new course got added.

Once that system existed, I handed its ongoing maintenance to Carlos and Aaron. That wasn't a step back from the work — it was possible because the hardest part was already done. Establishing the system, the logic behind it, and the relationship with CDC was mine; extending it into new courses was work I could responsibly delegate. The system is still what CDC's design work runs on today.

What CDC asked for next

CDC kept coming back.

When they needed a digital audit, the firm's accounts team came to me directly — CDC had made clear this was who they wanted for it. Over roughly three and a half years, that same pattern repeated: CDC brought me onto proposals I wasn't otherwise staffed on, and the engagements accumulated — the web app, the design system, a CFA evaluation, a data-modernization initiative, a formal development-approach recommendation, the audit.

None of that happened because I was chasing more CDC work. It happened because the first answer I gave them — in a room where nobody could technically evaluate it — turned out to be right, and everything after that was CDC continuing to bet on the same judgment.

The pattern underneath all of it was the same: find the part of the problem no one on the client side could evaluate themselves, do the translation work to make it legible, and only then build. That's what the original pitch was. It's what the design system became once I made it something Carlos and Aaron could extend without me. It's why CDC's account team asked for me by name for the audit instead of whoever was available. Different problems, same move.

The outcome

What began as "we need an app and don't know where to start" became roughly three and a half years of CDC repeatedly asking for me by name — first to build OneLab REACH, then to own its system, then for a digital audit, then for more.

I wouldn't attribute all of that momentum to any one deliverable. The more honest read is the sequence itself: a technical recommendation CDC could trust without being able to evaluate it themselves, a platform built on that recommendation, a system built to outlast me on the account — and it has. The components and patterns I designed are still what CDC's design work runs on.

“Fiona created a high quality final deliverable for our project that ‘wow’d’ our clients and built trust with them. This research and work will be the foundation of future development work with them for the coming 5 years...”

— Claire Cooper, CSELS Account Team Manager, CDC

They asked me to figure out how to build one thing they didn't understand.

They kept asking me back because I kept being right about the next one.

DOJ Time to Hire

A specialized tool to aid HR staff with complex workforce management cases

One hiring process, built to work across agencies that don't share a tech stack

Design Lead — Time to Hire, built on a design system created to hold across domain-separated DOJ organizations.

Client

U.S. Department of Justice, Office of the CIO

Role

Design Lead

Product

Time to Hire

Scope

OCIO design foundation: components, interaction patterns, accessibility standards

The Problem

DOJ's recruitment process ran manually across agencies that don't share a common technical environment — each domain-separated org has its own systems and constraints. Onboarding involved hundreds of staff hours spent chasing documents, re-entering the same information across disconnected steps, and manually assembling paperwork with no shared source of truth. Consolidating that into one process meant designing something that had to hold up across environments that weren't built to talk to each other.

My Role

I came in as Design Lead responsible for establishing OCIO's product design foundation — shared components, interaction patterns, and accessibility standards — that other teams could build on. Time to Hire was the first major feature built on top of that foundation, and I presented the direction, including this system, directly to DOJ executive leadership, including the CIO.

Approach

The foundation had to solve a harder problem than any single feature: create one common design language flexible enough to work across independently-run technical environments, without forcing every domain org onto identical infrastructure. I designed it to be cloned and adapted rather than assuming any org could just adopt it wholesale — the reuse model had to match how DOJ's orgs actually operate, separately.

Time to Hire applied that foundation to the recruitment process specifically: integrations, auto-populating smart fields, and in-place document generation replaced steps that previously required staff to gather the same information from multiple disconnected systems by hand. Generated documents could be downloaded pre-filled, reviewed copies uploaded back into the same flow, and a built-in checklist tracked what both staff and hiring managers still needed to provide — collapsing what had been a multi-system, manually-tracked process into one place.

Outcome

Time to Hire shipped as the first feature built on OCIO's new design foundation, consolidating a previously manual, multi-agency recruitment process into a single flow — and proving the underlying system could actually hold across DOJ's independently-run technical environments, not just work as a one-off feature.

DOJ Time to Hire

A specialized tool to aid HR staff with complex workforce management cases

Turning a hundred-step hiring process into one screen a specialist could actually work from

I joined DOJ's Office of the Chief Information Officer as Lead Product Designer to replace an employee portal built on, functionally, no design standard at all — seven different hex codes were circulating as "DOJ blue," and nobody could say which one was actually right. Time to Hire, a federal hiring workflow with hundreds of steps and multiple users, was the first feature to launch on the foundation I built to replace it.

Role

Lead Product Designer, Office of the Chief Information Officer

Scope

DOJ Employee Service Portal, shared design system, and custom features

Featured workflow

Time to Hire — delivered in four months

Team

Fiona Cohen, product design · Colin, engineering lead · Allison, communications and project coordination

The process had already outgrown paper, but not the habits that went with it

Federal hiring at DOJ ran through hundreds of steps and multiple people — recruiters, hiring managers, HR specialists — each responsible for a different segment of the same case. Before Time to Hire, that process lived across manual document handling: the same information re-typed into every required form, generated paperwork tracked outside any system, and no single view of what was done versus outstanding.

None of that was a UI problem waiting for a coat of paint. It was a workflow with no infrastructure behind it — built, until this point, on individual habit rather than a system anyone could rely on.

I designed Time to Hire to fix that. It also had to work on a foundation that didn't yet exist: DOJEMP itself had no established design system when I joined, and everything I built for Time to Hire had to hold up as other DOJ teams started building on the same base.

The main workspace: everything a case needed, in one place

Time to Hire needed to communicate where a recruitment case stood while giving staff access to the information and actions for the current segment — without forcing them out to email, a shared drive, or a separate tracker to get the rest of the job done.

The main workspace brought those layers together: recruitment progress across the top, the relevant form in the main area, and supporting documents alongside it. Staff assignments and case information stayed visible in the same view, so no one had to leave the workspace to understand who owned what.

The call I owned: a checklist would have left the real work to staff

In federal HR, forms are the job. Re-entering information, preparing documents, and tracking submissions create work around the process itself — and that work is where hours actually disappeared.

The proposed alternative for handling attachments was a static list of required documents. It would tell staff what they needed and stop there: retyping the same information into each document by hand, tracking what was done versus outstanding with no view of it inside the tool, and managing the files themselves somewhere else entirely. The list would name the requirement. It wouldn't touch any of the work behind it.

I pushed for a dynamic widget instead — one that combined the requirements with the actions: generate the document, download it for review, upload the reviewed version, and see what remained outstanding, all beside the relevant form.

Time and developer cost were the resistance. A static list was the smaller build. But the difference wasn't decorative: the dynamic version could pull from information already entered in the form and carry the document through its next step, instead of only naming it and leaving staff to do the rest by hand, outside the tool, on their own.

Colin and I got that version built. We kept the interaction inside a compact widget, giving staff more capability without creating a separate document-management workspace.

The document lifecycle, built as one sequence

The value came from the sequence working together — generation reducing repeated entry, download and upload supporting review, the checklist keeping what remained visible.

Generate from information already entered

Relevant form data populated the generated document, reducing how much staff had to fill in manually. The widget showed confirmation when generation succeeded, making the result of the action visible in place.

Download for review

Staff could download the pre-filled document directly from the widget. The document stayed associated with its form and recruitment segment, rather than becoming a disconnected task to locate and manage elsewhere.

Return the reviewed version

Reviewed files could be uploaded in the same location. Showing the filename and upload confirmation made it clear what had been attached and whether the action had completed.

Know what remains

The widget also acted as a document checklist for the current segment. Required files and their states appeared together, letting staff distinguish completed work from outstanding items without consulting a separate tracking surface.

The full interface also addressed completion of the form itself. The acknowledgement state explicitly warned that the form would no longer be editable after confirmation — the distinction between saving work and confirming completion stayed visible at the point of commitment.

Leading the design through a room full of opinions

I owned product design across the engagement. Colin led engineering, and Allison handled communications and project coordination. I presented both proposed designs and completed builds to DOJ leadership, including the CIO, for approval.

One of the hardest constraints was the number of stakeholders with strong opinions about the interface — color, modal interactions, and more. Not every decision matched the approach I would have chosen independently. Leading the design meant working within that approval environment while continuing to advocate for the details that affected people's work — the document widget among them. My partnership with Colin was central to getting that interaction beyond the proposal stage: I owned its design, he brought the engineering expertise to build it, and together we delivered it as part of the working product.

Why it could ship in four months: the foundation underneath it

None of the above happened on a blank slate, and it didn't happen in isolation either.

Getting DOJ to a single agreed hex code sounds trivial. It wasn't — not because the decision itself was hard, but because nobody had ever made anyone agree to anything before; every team had just picked their own and moved on. Once that was settled, I built a shared design system meant to hold up across DOJ's separated agencies, each with different needs but no reason to reinvent the basics every time. Teams could add their own widgets, adjust colors, adapt layouts — the system gave them a starting point, not a mandate to be identical.

That foundation is what let Time to Hire move as fast as it did. The recruitment-stage strip, the form patterns, the attachment components — none of them had to be invented from nothing, because the system to build them from already existed. And the reverse held too: other DOJ teams and agencies reused what I'd built for Time to Hire, adapting it for their own workflows without reinventing the basics themselves.

Outcome

Time to Hire shipped in four months and worked across agencies. The previous manual process was retired, and HR staff were required to use the replacement — a hundreds-of-steps, multiple-user workflow that went from re-typed forms and untracked documents to one workspace with generation, review, and tracking built in.

The foundation it shipped on outlived the project: other teams and agencies reused the design system, adapting it for their own organizational needs — the same foundation that started with getting people to agree on a single hex code.

Our team received an official Special Commendation in 2024 from the Office of the Chief Information Officer. I served as Lead Product Designer on that team.

Time to Hire is what the foundation was for. The commendation was for building both.

J.P. Morgan Asset Outage Modeling Tool

A simulation tool for visualizing outage impacts and asset dependencies

Modeling cascading infrastructure failure, grounded in one analyst's real workflow

Reporting & Analytics Lead — the Asset Outage Modeling Tool, built with a 3-person team over 8 months.

Client

J.P. Morgan Chase

Role

Reporting & Analytics Lead

Team

Bill Hess (Design Lead), Meghan Hensley (Disaster Management Specialist), Eric Albright (PM)

Duration

8 months

The Problem

J.P. Morgan Chase's disaster management team needed to model the impact of essential service outages and cascading failures across critical financial infrastructure — the kind of scenario planning that directly feeds emergency preparation and regulatory compliance. Without a purpose-built tool, that analysis relied on manual, calculation-heavy workflows that ate into the time analysts should have spent on the actual strategic judgment calls.

My Role

I served as Reporting and Analytics Lead, working end-to-end from strategy through scoping, design, and final delivery, on a team with a Design Lead (Bill Hess), a Disaster Management Specialist (Meghan Hensley), and a PM (Eric Albright). My workstream owned the reporting and analytics side of the tool and the client relationship for that stream directly.

Approach

I grounded the research in the disaster management team's actual workflow rather than assumptions: user interviews, workflow observation sessions, and a direct study of government disaster-readiness regulations, specifically to understand how dependencies between assets work in practice. That research surfaced a two-task core workflow — search across assets and compliance criteria, then map the interdependencies between them to expose cascading failure risk — and I used a concrete hypothetical scenario (modeling hurricane-season disruption to Florida's infrastructure) to keep every design decision tied to a real use case instead of an abstraction.

From that research, I made a specific interaction call: analysts needed to adjust their models without starting over, watching the impact of a criteria change in real time. That requirement drove a two-pane layout — criteria selection always visible on one side, the dependency view reacting live on the other — rather than a linear wizard-style flow that would have forced analysts to commit to criteria before seeing any impact. Because most analysts' actual workflow ended in Excel, I made data-table export a first-class action throughout, not an afterthought bolted onto the end.

J.P. Morgan Asset Outage Modeling Tool

A simulation tool for visualizing outage impacts and asset dependencies

Modeling cascading infrastructure failure, grounded in one analyst's real workflow

Reporting & Analytics Lead on a 3-person core team — 8 weeks to turn an unsolved modeling problem into a tool J.P. Morgan put in front of its own stakeholders.

Client

J.P. Morgan Chase

Role

Reporting & Analytics Lead

Team

Bill Hess (Design Lead), Meghan Hensley (Disaster Management Specialist), Eric Albright (PM)

Duration

8 weeks to delivery

The problem

J.P. Morgan's disaster management team needed to model how outages across critical financial infrastructure could cascade through the assets and services that depended on them — the kind of scenario planning that feeds directly into emergency preparation and regulatory compliance.

The problem had resisted an earlier internal attempt. The hard part wasn't simply visualizing complicated data. An asset could depend on one thing while simultaneously supporting another, with those relationships extending into increasingly complicated chains.

We had to translate that abstract, bidirectional dependency logic into something an analyst could actually use to make decisions under time pressure.

My role

I led Reporting & Analytics end-to-end — research, scoping, product decisions, design, delivery, and the direct client relationship — alongside Bill Hess, Design Lead for the broader ERMA platform; Meghan Hensley, Disaster Management Specialist; and Eric Albright, PM.

Bill owned design direction for ERMA as a whole. I owned the outage modeling and reporting experience specifically, working with Meghan to understand the domain, the product team to translate business requirements into priorities, and engineering to keep those decisions buildable.

That put me in the room translating disaster-management requirements into product decisions — and defending those decisions with the client directly.

Making the abstraction concrete

I couldn't design against "cascading infrastructure failure" as a concept, so I made it concrete.

I built one scenario: a hurricane disrupting Florida's critical infrastructure during the 2024 season.

Then I worked through it the way an analyst would: What would they search for? Which criteria mattered? What relationships would they need to inspect? What would they need to understand from the result to make a decision?

That exercise reduced a sprawling modeling problem to two core jobs:

Build the model. Understand its impact.

Those became the organizing principle for the product.

Turning complexity into product decisions

Working through the scenario exposed something the interface had to make intuitive: dependency wasn't one-directional.

Analysts needed to understand both what depended on an asset and what that asset depended on. Rather than create separate workflows for each direction, I collapsed that complexity into one control: View Dependents / View Dependent On.

That same requirement shaped the larger interaction model. Analysts needed to change their criteria and immediately understand the effect, so I pushed for a persistent two-pane experience instead of a linear wizard. Criteria remained visible while the resulting dependencies updated beside them, letting analysts refine a model without repeatedly starting over.

But simplifying the experience also meant deciding where not to spend engineering effort.

Multi-level dependency chains were expensive to support and weren't necessary to make the core workflow useful, so I kept them out of v1. And when analysts reached the point where they needed deeper manipulation of the data, I didn't recreate mature functionality they already had in Excel. I made structured export part of the workflow instead.

Spend complexity where it changes the analyst's job, not where it simply makes the product bigger.

J.P. Morgan Risk Management Dashboards

Designing dashboards for bank resiliency, planning, risk management, and compliance

Turning endless reporting requests into a system JPMC could actually afford to scale

Reporting & Analytics Lead for ERMA, J.P. Morgan Chase's enterprise resiliency and risk-management platform. I was brought onto the project specifically to lead reporting and analytics because of my background in data design.

Client

J.P. Morgan Chase

Role

Reporting & Analytics Lead

Product

ERMA — resiliency & risk-management platform

Engagement

1 year; 4 core dashboard areas; 1 reusable widget + modal system

The problem

J.P. Morgan Chase was replacing disaster management and resiliency planning software that had been in use since the 1990s with ERMA: a centralized platform for developing, implementing, and monitoring the bank's resiliency and risk-management plans.

This wasn't reporting for its own sake. Disaster management and resiliency planning is how a bank the size of JPMC protects customers, employees, and assets when something goes wrong — and it's also how the bank demonstrates to regulators that it's meeting its obligations. If ERMA's reporting layer couldn't keep pace with what resiliency specialists and risk teams needed to see, that wasn't a UI inconvenience. It was a gap in the bank's ability to show its own compliance.

Risk and compliance teams used ERMA's reporting directly — it wasn't a downstream summary someone translated later. Whatever the reporting layer could or couldn't show was what those teams had to work with.

Reporting and analytics had every reason to become unmanageable trying to meet that bar.

JPMC had a tremendous amount of data, limited engineering capacity, and a large group of business analysts, resiliency specialists, and other stakeholders who all had opinions about what the product should report. Requests changed constantly. New metrics appeared. Stakeholders asked for different graph types and different ways of representing essentially the same kinds of information.

The engineers responsible for building it were already overstretched.

I had been brought in specifically to lead Reporting & Analytics because data design was an area I was known for. My job wasn't simply to turn that backlog of requests into dashboards. I needed to figure out how much of that complexity the product actually needed to inherit.

If every reporting request became a bespoke design and engineering problem, ERMA would become increasingly expensive to build every time it grew — and the reporting a regulated bank depended on would be at the mercy of whatever engineering capacity happened to be available that quarter.

The apparent variety wasn't actually that varied

Once I looked across the reporting requirements, a pattern became obvious.

Despite stakeholders asking for many different graphs, the overwhelming majority of the data could be represented through a much smaller visualization vocabulary — primarily pie charts and histograms. Even data being requested in other formats could generally be communicated effectively through those same structures.

That changed the problem.

I didn't need to design an interface capable of accommodating an unlimited number of visualization types. I needed to design a small number of patterns flexible enough to accommodate a very large number of metrics.

So I deliberately constrained the system.

Rather than giving every request its own graph type, component, and implementation, I established a limited visualization vocabulary and designed the reporting architecture around it.

That was the central product decision: give JPMC flexibility in what it could report without creating infinite flexibility in how the software had to work.

Making a small system do a lot of work

Constraining the visualization vocabulary only worked if the resulting system could still handle the breadth and density of JPMC's reporting needs.

I designed reusable widgets that shared a common structure while supporting different metrics, visualizations, legends, values, labels, and states. Instead of engineering treating every new report as a new interface, the same underlying components could be configured around different data.

The system supported four major reporting areas:

— Information Hub — an inventory and summary of resiliency records

— Action Center — work requiring attention

— Measurement Viewer — reporting and performance metrics

— Activity Calendar — time-based resiliency activity

Across those dashboards, the information could change substantially without requiring the interaction model to change with it.

Designing the grammar, not just the screens

I defined the anatomy of the widgets so that variation happened inside intentional boundaries.

Titles, values, visualizations, legends, labels, supporting information, and actions each had a predictable place. Different configurations could accommodate meaningful differences in the data without requiring a new component every time someone requested a different metric.

This wasn't standardization for its own sake. Every additional visualization or layout pattern carried a real cost in design, development, testing, and maintenance. With limited engineering capacity and requirements that were going to continue changing, the architecture needed to absorb new requests rather than multiply with them.

The templates also gave us a way to respond to stakeholders without treating every preference as a new product requirement.

Instead of starting with, Which graph do you want?

I could start with, What are you actually trying to understand?

Then I could determine which existing pattern communicated it.

Constraint didn't mean removing the complexity

A small set of widgets was enough for monitoring. It wasn't enough for investigation.

Resiliency data could become extremely detailed, and users still needed to understand what sat behind a summary metric. I didn't want to solve the scalability problem by flattening the analytical experience.

So I designed the system in layers.

The dashboard handled the first question: What is happening?

Selecting a widget opened a reusable modal for the next questions: Why is it happening? What records are behind it? How can I narrow the data? What do I need to do with it next?

Within the modal, users could move from a larger visualization into the underlying records, refine the dataset with filters, work through detailed tabular information, and export data when they needed to continue analysis outside ERMA.

I standardized this layer too.

The visualization occupied a predictable region. Filters had a consistent home. Detailed records followed beneath the visualization. Actions remained in expected locations. The content could change without forcing users — or engineering — to relearn the structure.

This was the balance I was trying to maintain throughout Reporting & Analytics:

Constrain the number of patterns without constraining what users could learn from the data.

From a metric to the data behind it

The widget and modal systems were designed as one interaction model, not two collections of components.

A user could scan a dashboard, identify a metric that needed attention, open it for deeper analysis, refine the information, inspect the individual records behind it, and export the results when necessary.

That meant ERMA could support both quick situational awareness and much deeper analytical work without putting all of that complexity on the dashboard at once.

The result was an interface that could be information-dense without becoming structurally unpredictable. Users encountered the same basic grammar as they moved between different reporting needs, while the data — not a constantly changing UI — remained the variable.

Keeping the system intact in a high-pressure environment

The design problem wasn't happening in isolation.

ERMA had business analysts, resiliency specialists, product leadership, engineering, and other stakeholders deeply involved in the work. Requirements moved frequently, people had strong opinions about how information should be represented, and the project itself operated under significant pressure.

A reusable system only mattered if it survived that environment.

As Reporting & Analytics Lead, I had to understand the expertise behind people's requests without automatically translating every request into another feature or pattern. I worked across groups that didn't always agree with one another and became someone who could maintain productive relationships even with stakeholders other team members found difficult to work with.

That mattered to the product. Every request to make one dashboard behave differently could have weakened the architecture. Keeping the system coherent required both design judgment and the ability to explain why a constraint existed, understand what someone was actually trying to accomplish, and find a solution inside the system whenever possible.

The architecture gave us boundaries.

My job was to make those boundaries useful enough that the organization could work within them.

The system became the foundation

The constraint held.

Engineering reused the widget and modal templates, and they became the foundation for ERMA's dashboards as the platform continued to expand. New metrics did not automatically require new interface patterns. New reporting needs could be incorporated into an architecture the team already understood how to design, build, and use.

That mattered beyond the product itself. Risk and compliance teams were working directly from this reporting layer — a system that could absorb new disaster management and resiliency planning requirements without falling behind meant JPMC's ability to monitor and demonstrate its own compliance could keep pace with the bank's actual needs, not with however much engineering time happened to be available.

That was the outcome I had been designing toward from the beginning: ERMA could grow without its reporting interface — and the compliance work that depended on it — growing at the same rate as stakeholder requests.

The engagement grew too. My firm added two additional people to the contract, and the trust I built through ERMA led directly to my next J.P. Morgan assignment: the Asset Outage Modeling Tool.
