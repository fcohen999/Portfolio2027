The Problem

Sound Transit had already run an accessibility audit before I joined. It produced thousands of Jira tickets — and stalled there. Thousands of disconnected findings against a 7,000+ page site gave engineering no way to prioritize: every ticket looked equally urgent, and equally impossible to act on at that volume. The program needed a different frame, not more findings.

My Role

I came in as Accessibility Lead responsible for turning that audit into a strategy engineering could actually execute — which meant deciding what not to fix as much as what to fix. That's a scoping call, not a QA task: I was the one who decided the site's real accessibility risk lived in a few hundred pages and two dozen shared templates, not in exhaustively clearing every one of the original tickets.

Approach

I reduced the 7,000+ page site to the 160 highest-traffic pages and 29 shared templates that actually determined coverage — the insight being that fixing a handful of shared templates fixes every page that inherits from them, while chasing individual page tickets does not compound the same way.

To make that reframing operational rather than just a slide, I built four reusable applications covering audit collection, aggregation, deduplication, and analytics — turning thousands of scattered findings into structured data that could actually surface which issues were systemic versus one-off, and which fixes would move the most pages at once.

I also built a repeatable manual testing system: automating the routine, mechanical checks, defining testing protocols for what still needed a human, and creating a workflow a team member could run consistently under my direction — so the program didn't depend on me personally to keep generating findings.

From there I set a three-phase remediation strategy — shared components first, then templates, then isolated issues — which gave engineering an explicit order of operations instead of an undifferentiated backlog, and directed their effort toward the fixes with the greatest system-wide impact.

7,000+ pages → 160 pages / 29 templates

SCOPED TO THE COVERAGE THAT ACTUALLY MATTERED

Bringing Teams Along

None of this works if it's just a methodology I hand off. I brought the client and engineering teams around this approach — the shift from "clear every ticket" to "fix the templates that matter" is a hard sell to a team that's already been told the compliance risk is 7,000 pages deep — and then transferred the tooling, documentation, and training needed for them to continue independently, since the goal was a program that outlasted my engagement.

Outcome

The engagement moved Sound Transit from a stalled, unworkable audit to a scoped, prioritized, and tooled remediation program: a defined set of 160 pages and 29 templates instead of an undifferentiated 7,000+, four purpose-built tools instead of a spreadsheet of Jira tickets, and a three-phase plan the client's own team could execute and extend after I left.
